# Where Comcast is targeting HOAs and local governments

**Finding.** Comcast runs two national designs that show up in local records: Xfinity Communities contracts signed by association boards and property owners, and public partnerships with counties, states, and housing authorities. The cities below are the places a source names. They are examples, not a map of where Comcast is winning communities.

Retrieved 28 Sep 2026. The case repo (`PrimarySources`, `Decisions`, `Analysis`) has Charter franchise language and an FCC location-lead comparison of Comcast and Charter plant. It does not contain a Comcast HOA or local-government place list. Nothing in that location-lead file is used as evidence of HOA targeting.

## 1. HOA and community targeting

### What the offer is

Xfinity Communities is Comcast’s property channel. Its own pages sell customized internet to property managers and developers for both multi-dwelling buildings and single-family communities: bulk internet billed through the property, managed Wi-Fi across homes and common areas, and “Connected Building” service with pre-installed equipment, separate networks for residents, guests, and staff or devices, and 24/7 monitoring. Video can sit on top through the Xfinity Stream app, with an upgrade path to X1. New construction is assigned a Development Expansion Professional from groundbreaking; existing properties get a Community Account Representative.

Source: [Xfinity Communities — property management](https://www.xfinity.com/multifamily/property-owners) and [packages](https://www.xfinity.com/multifamily/products), retrieved 28 Sep 2026. Comcast’s multifamily brochure describes the same menu (bulk internet, managed Wi-Fi, smart-building tools) and says the infrastructure supports units nationwide. It cites a Parks Associates 2024 owner survey on bulk and managed Wi-Fi in the industry; that survey is industry context, not a Comcast city list. [Brochure PDF](https://www.xfinity.com/smartsolutions/documents/Achieving%20Turnkey%20Connectivity%20-%20Elevating%20The%20Total%20Multifamily%20Experience.pdf).

The property page also quotes a manager at “the Coda property” in the Denver area describing gigabit service as an amenity. That is Comcast marketing. No Coda contract, unit count, or date was found.

### Who signs

On the executed agreements below, the customer is the association, and the signatory is a board officer (president), not the resident. Comcast’s party is Comcast Cable Communications Management, LLC or an operating affiliate. Notice copies go to an Xfinity Communities desk (Florida examples: Boca Raton) and to cable legal in Philadelphia. Apartment and new-build sales, on the public site, are aimed at the owner, manager, or developer. A resident who wants bulk service is told to send the board to the multifamily contact form.

### What “targeting” means operationally

Posted contracts show a repeating bargain, not a one-time promotional mailer:

- **Bulk service.** The association pays a monthly per-unit fee for video and/or internet on every unit. Residents can upgrade; the association bill does not shrink if someone opts out. Equipment often stays the resident’s responsibility.
- **Wiring control.** The company takes exclusive use of home-run wiring and non-exclusive use of in-unit home wiring.
- **Exclusive marketing.** The association agrees to present Comcast materials at leasing or closing, keep them in the office, and allow on-site sales events, and not to market a similar service. Door-to-door sales are sometimes barred unless the association consents (Pearland and Hollywood examples).
- **A fee paid to the association.** Recent posted deals use a one-time “per unit compensation,” often $100 per unit, paid after a W-9, with a refund if the association defaults. Older posted deals use $150 and $200 per unit. This is consideration for access and the contract, not an annual royalty.
- **Common-area service.** Courtesy TV or internet outlets, and in at least one 2025 contract a small common-area Wi-Fi build where Comcast already has plant.

The same form is used for condominiums and for at least one single-family HOA (Pearland). Florida statute lets boards contract for communications as a common expense and lets residents keep access to other franchised providers, which is why a bulk contract and “exclusive marketing” can coexist with a resident’s right to buy from someone else directly. That legal point is Florida association law, summarized by a law-firm explainer, not a Comcast statement. [Yates Anderson, retrieved 28 Sep 2026](https://yatesanderson.com/library/florida-bulk-cable-internet-pass-throughs).

An Illinois townhome resident described a 10-year “Xfinity Communities” renewal that paid about $100 per unit for exclusive easement and advertising and did not include community-wide service. The post does not name the community. It is a forum description of a marketing-and-access contract, not a second geography. [r/HOA, retrieved 28 Sep 2026](https://www.reddit.com/r/HOA/comments/1mdfxb8/ilth_information_on_comcastxfinity_community/).

Comcast’s fiscal 2025 Form 10-K does not break out bulk, MDU, or community-association revenue. Franchise-fee language in that filing is about cable franchises, not HOA door fees.

## 2. Local government targeting

Three motions are visible. They should stay separate.

### A. Franchise footprint

Comcast’s cable systems sit in public rights-of-way under franchises. The fiscal 2025 Form 10-K says federal, state, and local franchising rules can require public, educational, and governmental access channels, renewal procedures, franchise fees, and customer-service standards. Network plant is also described as sitting on owned and leased property and under agreements with local utilities and municipalities. That is how the existing footprint is authorized. It is not, by itself, a campaign aimed at governments.

One executed renewal illustrates the ordinary motion. Clark County, Washington, and Comcast Cable Communications Management, LLC, entered a nonexclusive cable franchise effective 1 Jul 2024 through 30 Jun 2034, succeeding a 2013–2023 franchise. The county document is a right-of-way renewal for cable service, including public-access obligations, not a broadband-expansion sale. [County franchise materials posted by the City of Vancouver](https://www.cityofvancouver.us/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/2024-05-01_County_Franchise_Ordinance_-_County_and_Comcast_Signed_compressed.pdf).

The 10-K also lists municipal broadband networks and municipal utilities as competitors, and notes that some states restrict municipal networks while others make them easier. It does not name cities where Comcast is running an opposition campaign. No such city list was found in the primary pages reviewed. [Comcast Form 10-K for the year ended 31 Dec 2025, filed on EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1166691/000162828026004994/cmcsa-20251231.htm).

### C. Housing authorities and public or affordable housing

Here the buyer is a housing authority or an affordable-housing operator, sometimes with a city, county, or state paying. Residents are not the contracting party.

**St. Paul Public Housing Agency, Minnesota.** On 11 Jun 2024 the PHA and Comcast marked pre-installed connectivity in PHA hi-rises. The PHA’s account says each unit receives a free modem at no cost to the PHA or the tenant, starting at Central and Montreal hi-rises, with 14 more hi-rises to follow. The PHA called itself the first housing authority in Minnesota in this arrangement with Comcast. Comcast’s speaker was a Midwest region vice president. This is a housing-authority deployment, not an HOA board vote. [PHA news](https://mailchi.mp/stpha/pha-news-june2024).

**Marin Housing Authority, California.** A 23 Jul 2024 agreement between Marin Housing Authority and the county’s information-services department says MHA had already contracted with Xfinity Comcast for broadband at Kruger Pines, Homestead Terrace, Golden Hinde, Venetia Oaks, and Casa Nova. The county uses ARPA funds to pay MHA, which pays Comcast, up to $20 per month per interested resident for two years. The county department is not a party to the Comcast contract. [County metaviewer copy](https://marin.granicus.com/MetaViewer.php?clip_id=12296&meta_id=1333807&view_id=59).

**Massachusetts Residential Retrofit Program.** On 6 Aug 2025 the Massachusetts Broadband Institute awarded $31,548,018 across four providers for more than 13,700 units. Comcast Cable Communications’ slice is **$12,932,384 for 5,179 units**. The state release says Comcast offered income-eligible plans of at least 100/100 Mbps, common-area Wi-Fi, on-site digital-navigation training through a third party, and open access to in-building wiring. Open access is a different wiring bargain from the exclusive home-run terms in the Florida and Texas association contracts. Funding is U.S. Treasury Capital Projects Fund money under ARPA.

### D. Community Wi-Fi and Lift Zones

Lift Zones are free Wi-Fi and devices at community sites, funded as part of Comcast’s Project UP commitment. Comcast says the program launched in 2020, passed 1,250 locations, and carries a $50 million cash and in-kind commitment for flagship sites. Flagship sites are described as powered by Comcast Business. The host is usually a nonprofit, not a city procurement.

Comcast’s 1 Oct 2025 release names five flagships that season: Aspire Center, Chicago (Austin); Building Bridges Across the River at TheARC, Washington, D.C.; Opportunities Industrialization Center, Philadelphia; Hmong American Partnership, St. Paul; El Centro de la Raza, Federal Way. It points back to 2024 flagships in Atlanta, Houston, and Oakland, and ahead to more flagships by the end of 2026, including Pennsylvania and Florida, without naming those cities. The D.C. site is tied to a $1 million Comcast commitment as connectivity partner for the 11th Street Bridge Park, described as a joint initiative with the District Department of Transportation. That is the one flagship in the release with a named local-government partner. [Comcast release, 1 Oct 2025](https://corporate.comcast.com/press/releases/comcast-expands-access-technology-wifi-launch-five-new-flagship-lift-zones).

A separate 18 Sep 2024 Comcast South post announces a Lift Zone at the Liberty County Boys & Girls Club in Hinesville, Georgia. The counterparty in that post is the club. [Comcast South](https://south.comcast.com/2024/09/18/comcast-announces-newest-lift-zone-at-the-liberty-county-boys-girls-club/).

Schools show up as E-Rate applicants that can select Comcast Business. This report does not turn a funding-wave printout into a list of targeted districts or contract values. No sourced smart-city platform sale or public-safety network contract was found in the pages reviewed.

## 5. What this tells Charter

The channel next to housing churn is real and specific. Comcast sells the association or the owner a multi-year bulk bill, control of home-run wiring, and exclusive on-site marketing, and it pays the association a one-time per-unit fee to take that deal. A board officer signs. The resident is not the customer of record for the bulk tier.

A second channel sits next to community and government relationships, and it is not the same sale. Counties and states co-fund builds to unserved or underserved addresses, and Comcast keeps the network. Housing authorities and affordable operators buy in-building connectivity, sometimes with a public subsidy of the resident bill (Marin) or a free modem (St. Paul’s own description). Lift Zones put Comcast Business Wi-Fi in community buildings and, in one D.C. case, next to a city transportation project.

Whether any of that overlaps Charter’s housing-driven Internet losses is a hypothesis. None of these sources says Comcast is taking Spectrum households through an HOA or housing-authority deal. The association contracts that name an address are inside communities Comcast is already serving. The expansion contracts are written as builds to addresses that lacked service. Charter’s own bulk-MDU counting rules in its earnings releases describe how Charter counts bulk units. They do not identify a Comcast win.

## 6. Unwilling to claim

- That the six association properties, or Florida, are where Comcast concentrates HOA sales. They are where a contract or audit was posted.
- A national count of communities, units under bulk, door-fee spend, or revenue from Xfinity Communities. The 2025 10-K does not disclose it.
- That exclusive marketing removes a resident’s ability to buy another provider. Florida association law, and several contracts, keep those ideas separate.
- Any city where Comcast is campaigning against a municipal broadband network. The 10-K names the competitor class only.
- That a cable-franchise renewal is a targeted government campaign. Clark County is included as the ordinary right-of-way motion.
- Smart-city or public-safety contract cities, values, or vendors. None were found in the sources above.
- School-district targeting lists or E-Rate dollar totals. A funding-wave file is not used here.
- That Lift Zone cities are government buyers. The named hosts are community organizations, except the D.C. bridge-park reference to DDOT.
- Staff numbers, win rates, or “Comcast is ahead of Spectrum in HOAs.” The Denver Coda line is a testimonial without a contract.
- The anonymous Illinois townhome community as a place. The post never names it.
- That Madison County’s grant-application ceiling, county contribution, and Comcast contribution add to one funding stack. They are stated separately in the agreement and are reported that way.
- That Stafford’s 600, 650, and “nearly 700” figures are one number. Each is tied to the source that printed it.
