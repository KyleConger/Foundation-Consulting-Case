# Class Notes: Core Takeaways from the Drinkware Exercise

Practice A (framestorm) and Practice B (commit to a number), Sept 8 2026. Each bullet is the
transferable rule plus the moment in our own work that proved it.

## Framing

- **If your three framings are variations on the first, you accepted its frame.** The fix is to
  *relocate* the problem, not reword the question — move it to the cupboard, the planogram, the
  factory and port, the courtroom, the cap table. Rewording produces self-similar frames that all
  fail together.

- **Interrogate the constraint before working.** "No market data" turned out to mean "I am not
  supplying data," not "do not use data." A negative constraint is worth one clarifying question,
  and asking whether the output is a **draft or an answer** changes what you build.

- **Strong frames contradict each other.** If all three can be true at once, they are a checklist
  (desirability / access / viability), not rival diagnoses. Frames that can't be wrong can't be
  tested.

- **Classify perspectives by role on one dimension only** — root frame, rival mechanism, hard
  constraint, sub-branch, decision architecture. Mixing "how good is it" with "what job does it do"
  produced the muddled first assessment. The "founder's right to win" frame only earned root status
  once it had to name an asset a competitor cannot buy or copy; as originally written it absorbed
  brand, channel, IP, and CAC, which made it unfalsifiable.

- **Don't grade your own homework.** When every frame I generated first survived my own review, that
  was anchoring, not quality. Running three models independently and *forbidding* reuse of prior runs
  produced genuinely different frames; the right move afterward was to reclassify all nine by role and
  rebuild one set from the parts, not to pick a winner.

## Sizing

- **A frame that doesn't produce its own number did no work.** Forcing each frame to yield its own
  serviceable market is what surfaced the finding: the B2B decorated pool ($681M) is **4.3× the
  consumer hygiene niche** ($158M). The frames disagreeing about size *was* the answer, and it changed
  the recommendation from "build a brand" to "sell where brand is not the buying criterion."

- **Commit to a point estimate, and round in the direction that costs you.** We committed $1.25B/yr
  rounded down from a $1,279.5M midpoint. A range alone is not a decision.

- **Two builds agreeing is not validation if they share an assumption.** Bottom-up ($1,327M) and
  top-down ($1,232M) landed 7.5% apart, but both route through an estimated $35–$50 band share, so
  they are **correlated by construction**. Agreement proved the arithmetic was consistent, not that
  the answer was right. A real cross-check shares no inputs — the year-3 figure had one (units × ASP
  = $4.43M vs. share-of-SAM = $4.40M).

- **Label geography and period on every single line.** Nearly every retrieved figure was global
  (YETI drinkware, e-commerce share) or North American (the promo channel), and each conversion to US
  had to be its own flagged line. The top-down build was US-only and annual in substance but said so
  nowhere — an unlabeled number gets misread later.

- **Precision migrates toward available data, not important data.** The two inputs I derived most
  carefully from SEC filings moved the answer **~1%**. The four pure team estimates with no retrieved
  anchor moved it **19–29%**. Rigor went where the data was easy, and the sensitivity ranking is what
  exposed it.

- **Size is not opportunity.** $1.25B looks like a market until channel contribution runs: at $40
  retail, DTC contribution is $17.14 against a $25–40 cold acquisition cost, so the first order loses
  money. The recommendation has to follow from contribution, not from market size.

## Process discipline

- **Recompute your own chain in code before shipping.** A $39 blended ASP was carried into the year-3
  line while the stated channel mix implied **$34.60** — a 13% overstatement ($5.0M vs $4.4M) that
  read fine in prose and survived into several documents. Nothing catches this except redoing the
  arithmetic.

- **Never let two live documents carry different headline numbers.** The correction lived in the audit
  file and the canvas but not the memo, so the deliverable contradicted itself. State corrections
  plainly and mark the superseded version.

- **Sequence work by leverage ÷ cost, cheapest binary gates first.** The customs-broker ruling on the
  duty stack costs $1–2K and under two weeks, and a Section 232 finding would add ~$4/unit and void a
  $35–$50 price point on its own. That outranks the $15K market study that only moves the estimate.

- **Write the kill rule and the "unwilling to claim" list before the recommendation**, so the analysis
  can't be quietly rationalized into a yes. Also name **who funded any survey** — the 85% ownership
  figure came from a survey commissioned by a bottle startup, and a second source said 60%.
